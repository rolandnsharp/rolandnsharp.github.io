# The Neuromancer

A new category of worker has arrived. Not a programmer exactly. Not a
manager. Something in between that didn't exist two years ago.

The neuromancer jacks into an AI coding assistant and steers. They don't
write most of the code. They decide what gets built, evaluate whether
it's working, and course-correct when it isn't. The terminal is the
cyberdeck. The AI is the construct. The imagination fills in the rest.

---

## What the Job Actually Is

The neuromancer's day looks like this:

Read something — a paper, a codebase, a problem description. Form an
idea of what should exist that doesn't yet. Open a terminal. Start
talking.

The AI writes code. The neuromancer reads it, runs it, watches what
happens. Something breaks. The neuromancer describes the failure. The AI
fixes it. Something works but feels wrong. The neuromancer says why. The
AI adjusts. Over hours and days, a system takes shape that neither could
have built alone — the AI lacks direction, the human lacks
implementation speed, but together they move.

This is not "using a tool." A hammer doesn't propose alternatives when
you miss the nail. This is a collaboration with something that has read
every textbook but has no taste, no instinct for what matters, no sense
of when to stop. The neuromancer provides that.

---

## The Skills

What makes a good neuromancer isn't what you'd expect.

**Taste.** Knowing which ideas are worth pursuing. An AI will happily
build anything you ask for. Most of what you could ask for is garbage.
The skill is filtering — knowing which architectures fit the problem,
which features are essential, which are noise.

**Evaluation.** Looking at output and knowing whether it's right. Not
just "does it compile" — does it do what it should? Is the text coherent?
Are the weights moving in the right direction? Is the performance
acceptable? The AI can't evaluate its own work. That's your job.

**Direction.** Keeping a multi-week project on track across dozens of
sessions. Remembering what was tried, what failed, what's next. The AI
starts fresh every conversation. The neuromancer is the continuity.

**Reading code you didn't write.** Every line the AI produces, you have
to be able to read. Not necessarily write it from scratch — but read it,
understand what it's doing, spot when it's wrong. This is a different
skill from writing code, and in some ways harder.

**Knowing what you don't know.** The neuromancer operates at the edge of
their understanding. They build systems whose internals they only
partially follow. The key is knowing which parts you don't understand
and asking until you do — or at least until you understand enough to
evaluate the output.

---

## What It's Not

It's not prompt engineering. Prompt engineering is writing careful
instructions for a single-shot response. The neuromancer is running a
multi-session project. Context management, architectural memory,
recognizing when the AI is confidently wrong — those aren't prompting
skills.

It's not no-code. The neuromancer reads and reviews every line. They
modify code, debug outputs, trace failures through systems. They need
to understand programming deeply enough to evaluate it, even if they're
not writing most of it.

It's not management. A manager delegates to people who have their own
judgment. The AI has knowledge but not judgment. The neuromancer
provides the judgment. Every decision flows through them.

---

## The Uncomfortable Part

There's a question that hangs over all of this: are you a fraud?

You built a 9.6 million parameter language model with temporal
difference learning and a Forth interpreter. You directed every
architectural decision. You read every line. But you didn't write most
of it. An AI did.

The answer depends on what you think "building" means. The architect
doesn't lay bricks. The director doesn't operate the camera. The
conductor doesn't play the violin. Nobody calls them frauds. They're
valued precisely because they know what should be built, not because
they build it with their own hands.

The neuromancer is the same thing, arrived at from a different direction.
The tool is new. The role is ancient.

---

## The Market

Companies don't know they need this yet. Job listings still say "5 years
Python" and "experience with React." They're hiring for the last
decade's skills.

But the companies that figure it out first will move faster. A
neuromancer with an AI assistant can explore architectural spaces that
would take a traditional team weeks. They can prototype in languages
they don't know, investigate approaches they haven't tried, build
systems at the edge of their competence and push that edge forward
every day.

The job title doesn't exist yet. The role does. The people filling it
are calling themselves "AI-augmented engineers" or "independent
researchers" or just "developers who use Claude a lot." Eventually
there'll be a real name for it.

For now: neuromancer.
